The 5.33% Summer

In two months the U.S. 30-year Treasury yield climbed to 5.33% — its highest since June 2007 — while the S&P 500 sat within about 1% of a record. What sold off, why the long end led, and how to tell a Fed repricing from a fiscal & term-premium repricing.

Window: Jun 30 → Aug 28, 2026 · Sources: CNBC, Bloomberg, U.S. Treasury, FRED / NY Fed, BLS, BEA, CBO, CRFB, TIPS Watch, Advisor Perspectives & others (linked below)

The short answer

  • What happened: the whole curve sold off, but the long end led — 30Y +24bp to 5.17% (peak 5.33% on Aug 18, a 19-year high), 10Y +29bp to 4.73%, 2Y +19bp to 4.34%, and the curve bear-steepened (2s10s 29→39bp; 30Y–2Y peaked +38bp steeper mid-August).
  • It was a real-yield move, not an inflation-expectations move: 10Y breakevens ended flat (~2.34%); 10Y real yields rose ~24bp; long TIPS auctions cleared at the highest real yields since 2001–08.
  • Two engines, not one: a genuine hawkish Fed repricing (June dots erased a cut; the July FOMC saw three hike dissents; Warsh's Jackson Hole speech put Sept-hike odds at 58%) plus a fiscal / term-premium repricing (ACM term premium +~35bp; $432B July deficit; $40T debt; costliest 30Y auction since 2001; Treasury doubled buybacks to calm the long end).
  • The tell: in Aug 7–18 the front end rallied on a −23k payrolls print while 30s made 19-year highs — that divergence is term premium, not the Fed.
  • It was global: UK 30Y highest since 1998, 30Y JGB above 4% for the first time ever, German 30Y funding costliest since 2011, Korea's curve steepest since 2021 — a synchronized long-end repricing.

Five terms, in plain words

Basis point (bp)
0.01%. A move from 5.00% to 5.33% is +33bp.
Real yield
The yield after expected inflation — what TIPS (inflation-protected bonds) pay. It compensates for growth and the price of capital.
Breakeven inflation
Nominal yield minus real yield: the inflation rate the market is pricing in.
Term premium
The extra yield investors demand just for holding a long bond instead of rolling short ones — compensation for duration, fiscal and inflation uncertainty, and who has to absorb the supply.
Bear steepener
Yields rise and long yields rise faster than short ones. Classic signature of supply / term-premium / inflation-risk moves rather than pure Fed moves.

AWhat happened

Start with the levels. On June 30 the curve read 2Y 4.15%, 10Y 4.44%, 30Y 4.93%. Yields ground higher all July — the 30-year crossed 5% in the first week and 5.20% by the Fed's July 29 meeting — then peaked in mid-August: the 30-year touched 5.33% on August 18, its highest since June 2007, before a surprise Treasury buyback announcement and a late hawkish twist at Jackson Hole reshaped the move. The window closed at 2Y 4.34%, 10Y 4.73%, 30Y 5.17%.

The curve: a bear steepener that partially unwound

The move was a textbook bear steepening until the last ten days. 2s10s widened from ~29bp (Jun 30) to ~44bp (Aug 27); the 30Y–2Y spread went from ~78bp to a peak of ~115bp on Aug 18 — that is, the long bond sold off ~38bp more than the 2-year at the extreme. Two late events then compressed it again: Treasury's Aug 19 buyback expansion pulled the long end in ~10bp, and Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole debut (Aug 28) spiked the front end (+6bp intraday on the 2Y) while 30s actually fell. Net-net the curve still ended ~5–15bp steeper.

Real yields did all the work; breakevens did nothing

Decompose the 10-year: nominal +24bp (Jun 30 → Aug 27), real yield +~24bp (an implied ~2.10% → 2.34% on FRED's DFII10), breakeven ~0bp (2.34% → 2.34%). The 30-year is the same story: real +~29bp (2.69% → ~2.98%), breakeven roughly −2bp. The primary-market evidence is striking: the July 23 10-year TIPS auction cleared at a 2.438% real yield, the highest since October 2008, and the Aug 20 30-year TIPS reopening at 2.973%, the highest since 2001. Longer-run inflation expectations stayed anchored throughout — 5y5y forward ~2.3%, Michigan 5–10yr at 3.3% and falling — despite the oil shock (Section B). Whatever drove this, it was not the market raising its long-run inflation forecast.

The global scoreboard: everyone's long end broke something

This was not an American story. The week of August 17–18 alone produced multi-decade milestones on four continents' curves — strong evidence that a global duration risk premium, not any single central bank, was repricing. The UK was the worst performer (fiscal politics under new PM Andy Burnham); Japan's super-long bonds set all-time records even with its policy rate at just 1%; Korea was the outlier whose curve steepened for the opposite front-end reason — its central bank is hiking into a semiconductor boom.

So where was the move concentrated?

The long end and the belly. The 3-month bill (+10bp, mechanically tracking a Fed that never moved) and the 2-year (+19bp, all of it in swings around data) moved least; the 10s and 30s moved most, the 30-year made the historic highs, the 20s and 30s were where auctions tailed, and long TIPS were where records fell. The one caveat: the 2-year's +19bp is not nothing — it repriced violently around events (Section B) — so "long-end concentrated" is right, "front end anchored" is not.

BWhat caused it

1 · Fed expectations: a real hawkish repricing — but of hikes, not cuts

The Fed held at 3.50–3.75% all summer (its fifth-plus consecutive hold), but the expected path moved a lot, and in one direction: hawkish. The June SEP erased the projected 2026 cut (median dot 3.8%). The oil spike of July 23 briefly put ~two hikes in the futures curve (Sept ~82%, Dec ~92%). Soft labor data then unwound most of it — down to ~42–46% after the −23k payrolls and an in-line CPI — before Warsh's Jackson Hole speech snapped Sept-hike odds back to ~58% on the window's final day. A September cut priced at roughly 1% throughout August; prediction markets put 57% odds on zero cuts in all of 2026. The 2-year's +19bp and the round-trip in hike odds are a genuine monetary-policy repricing — enough to explain perhaps half of the 10-year's move, but not the long end's outperformance to the downside, and not the mid-August episode when the front end was rallying while 30s made highs.

2 · Inflation, oil and the Iran shock: big headlines, anchored expectations

The backdrop is the 2026 Iran war (from February) and the Strait of Hormuz crisis: CPI peaked at 4.2% y/y in May. Within our window the shock re-escalated twice — US strikes in early July, then the July 23 tanker attack that drove Brent +7% in a day to $100.69 — and the ceasefire memorandum expired unrenewed on Aug 17 (Brent ~$88–94 late August; gasoline had its most expensive August on record at a $4.06 average). The prints themselves were two-sided: June CPI fell 0.4% m/m; July CPI was in line at 3.4%; July core PCE was hot at 3.3%.

Here's the crucial subtlety: oil moved near-term Fed pricing (that ~82% hike-odds spike) and household 1-year expectations (4.3% Michigan), but long-run market expectations never budged — 10Y breakeven flat at ~2.3%, 5y5y forward ~2.3%, Michigan 5–10yr easing to 3.3%. So the oil shock raised long yields mostly indirectly, by hardening the "no cuts, maybe hikes" path and by adding to inflation uncertainty (a term-premium input) — not by raising the inflation the market actually prices for the next decade.

3 · Growth: strong enough to carry higher real yields

The economy was bifurcated. Payrolls looked broken (−23k in July, −103k revisions) — but the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% because participation hit a 5-year low: a shrinking-labor-supply story, with jobless claims near 60-year lows (~203k). Meanwhile the demand side ran hot: Atlanta Fed GDPNow for Q3 sat at 4–6%, ISM manufacturing hit 55.6 (best since May 2022), the August flash composite PMI 56.0, and consumer spending grew 3.4% in Q2. Equities agreed — 27 S&P 500 record closes in 2026, the latest (7,798.99) on Aug 13, ending the window ~1% off the high. Strong growth with a supply-constrained labor market is exactly the mix that supports higher real yields — and it removes "growth scare" from the list of explanations. Yields did not rise because the economy was weak; they rose while it was strong.

4 · Fiscal deficit & Treasury supply: the long end's specific problem

July's Monthly Treasury Statement (Aug 12) showed a $432B monthly deficit — the largest since March 2021; the 10-month FY2026 deficit ran ~$1.80T, ~10% above FY2025, and CBO raised its FY2026 projection to ~$2.1T. A key twist: tariffs ran in reverse — after the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs (Feb 2026), Treasury paid ~$100B in refunds and July net customs receipts were negative $8.55B, blowing a ~$250B hole in expected 2026 revenue. Gross debt crossed $40 trillion on Aug 18, months early. Supply pressed on the exact sector that was breaking: the Aug 13 30Y auction cleared at 5.216%, the costliest since 2001; 20Y auctions tailed twice (graded D+ in July); Q3 borrowing was revised up $68B to $739B. Treasury's response — holding coupon sizes, leaning on bills ("T-bill and chill"), and then doubling long-end buybacks on Aug 19 — is itself the strongest evidence that officials saw long-end demand as fragile. The buyback rally fizzled in two days.

5 · Term premium: the measured wedge

The NY Fed's ACM 10-year term premium averaged 0.513% in June and 0.87% in July — a ~35bp jump, roughly the size of the entire net 10Y move — and stood at 0.80% in the latest daily print (Aug 13; the SF Fed's alternative model reads higher, ~1.33%). That is the wedge between "what the Fed does" and "what long bonds yield." Strategists said it plainly: Barclays called the rise "less about inflation and more about the U.S. budget deficit, high levels of issuance related to AI competing with Treasurys, and higher term premiums." The episode rhymes with October 2023's "term-premium tantrum" (10Y to 4.99% on deficit fears) far more than with any Fed cycle.

6 · AI capex: the new demand for capital

The most 2026-specific driver: the same AI boom holding equities at records is now competing with the Treasury for savings. The four big hyperscalers guided to roughly $725B of combined 2026 capex (vs ~$410B in 2025, +77%), funded increasingly with debt: US corporate bond issuance ran ~$1.7T year-to-date, +27% y/y, with hyperscaler issuance alone ~$159B by mid-2026 (vs a $28B/yr 2020–24 average) and Morgan Stanley projecting ~$570B of global AI-related debt in 2026. Barclays' arithmetic: AI-linked corporate supply added +$474B of net long-dated paper — more than offsetting a $440B drop in net Treasury coupon supply. A huge, credit-worthy borrower class bidding for the same duration buyers raises the price of long-term capital — that is, real yields and term premium. It's also the resolution of the equity–bond paradox: record stocks and a 5.3% long bond are the same shock (an investment boom) showing up with opposite signs in the two markets.

7 · Global spillovers: everyone's fiscal story at once

Japan's super-long selloff (30Y JGB above 4% for the first time, on PM Takaichi's fiscal expansion, BOJ tightening, and a yen so weak it took the first joint US–Japan intervention since 1998 to slow it) mechanically raises hedged global yields and pulls Japanese capital home. The UK added a political fiscal premium (30Y gilts at reported 1998 highs under the new Burnham government); Germany issued record debt for defense at its costliest 30Y levels since 2011; France's OAT–Bund spread hit its widest since 2012. When every major issuer's long end cheapens together, no single market can rally alone — the correlation itself became a driver.

The real question: Fed repricing or term-premium repricing?

If this were purely a monetary-policy repricing…

  • ✓ partlyFront end sells off as hikes get priced — 2Y +19bp, hike odds up; real, but modest
  • ✗Long end moves less than the belly (policy fades with horizon) — instead the 30Y led at every extreme
  • ✗Yields track each data print tightly — Barclays: supply pressures "overwhelm individual soft-data releases"
  • ✗Term-premium models stay flat — ACM jumped ~35bp

If this were a fiscal / term-premium repricing…

  • ✓Bear steepening, long end leads — 30Y–2Y +38bp steeper at the peak
  • ✓Real yields rise, breakevens don't — +24–29bp real, ~0bp breakeven; record TIPS auction yields
  • ✓Primary market strains: tails, high dealer takedown — two 20Y tails; costliest 30Y since 2001; Treasury buybacks doubled
  • ✓Global long ends move together — US / UK / Japan / Germany milestones in the same 48 hours

Verdict: a joint repricing — and you can see the seam

The summer move was both things at once, in different parts of the curve. The front end and belly carried a genuine hawkish policy repricing (worth roughly half the 10-year's net move, on a reasonable pass-through of the 2Y's +19bp). The long end carried that plus a distinct fiscal/term-premium repricing — the ACM jump, the flat breakevens against record real yields, the auction tails and the buyback intervention are its fingerprints.

The cleanest natural experiment is August 7–18: payrolls printed −23k, the 2-year rallied to ~4.17, hike odds fell — a dovish impulse — and the 30-year still ran to a 19-year high of 5.33%. A pure Fed story cannot produce that divergence; a supply/term-premium story predicts it. And the mirror image on August 28 confirms the split: Warsh's hawkish speech hit the 2-year (+6bp) while the 30-year fell — policy news now moves the front of the curve, while the long end trades the deficit, the supply calendar, and its buyer base.

Finally, the market told you what this wasn't: with equities ~1% from records, credit spreads near multi-decade tights (IG ~80bp, HY ~275–281bp) and breakevens asleep, this was neither a growth scare nor an inflation de-anchoring. It was the price of duration going up — because the sellers of duration (governments, and now the AI build-out) multiplied faster than its buyers.

+Other macro currents worth naming

The Fed-independence fight

Kevin Warsh took the chair in May 2026 after a party-line confirmation; the July FOMC's 9–3 hike dissents doubled as a credibility protest. The Supreme Court (5–4, June 29) blocked the firing of Governor Lisa Cook, with litigation continuing — and the White House kept publicly demanding lower rates. An institutional risk premium is plausibly inside that term-premium number.

The Bessent put (fiscal dominance?)

Treasury doubling long-end buybacks on Aug 19 — explicitly after a 19-year-high yield print — was read by some as debt-management hygiene and by others as the fiscal authority doing yield-curve maintenance the central bank won't. The rally lasted two days; the debate will last longer.

First joint yen rescue since 1998

Japan's MOF (~$60B) and the US Treasury jointly bought yen in early August after 163.73/$. It worked for a week — the yen gave back half the gains — but it marked officialdom's fear of the weak-yen → JGB-selloff → global-long-end loop.

Credit didn't blink

IG spreads ~80–81bp (near 1998 tights) and HY ~275–281bp (2007-era tights) despite record IG supply. The bond market's stress was entirely in the rates leg, not the credit leg — consistent with a supply/term-premium story, not a recession trade.

Gold high, dollar round-trip

Gold traded ~$4,400–4,500/oz in late August (a 3-month high; below January's $5,589 war-shock record). The DXY rose to ~101.4 by late July on hawkish repricing, slid to ~98.6 into the yen intervention and Fed drama, then bounced to ~99.6 on Warsh's speech.

The tariff refund hole

The February Supreme Court ruling against the IEEPA tariffs turned a revenue line into an outflow: ~$100B refunded, three straight months of negative net customs receipts, ~$250B off 2026 revenue projections — a direct feed into the deficit-and-supply story above.
